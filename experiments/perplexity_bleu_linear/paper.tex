\section{Introduction}

A central question for multilingual large language models is the extent
to which translation quality is determined by per-language competence
alone, as opposed to language-pair-specific factors. We frame this as a
specific hypothesis:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1.} Translation BLEU between an ordered language pair $(i, j)$
is predictable from per-language monolingual competence on $i$ and $j$,
without a substantive language-pair-specific interaction term.
\end{quote}

We address H1 via a per-language decomposition of pairwise BLEU scores
into source and target effects, and we test whether those effects are
predicted by monolingual competence proxies and whether any
pair-specific residual is detectable.

Our results support H1:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An additive random-effects model
    $\code{BLEU}_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_j + \varepsilon_{ij}$
    explains $86$--$93\%$ of centered BLEU variance across two models.
    A multiplicative (rank-1) decomposition fits comparably, but does not
    outperform additive on the same parameter budget.
  \item The recovered language effects $\alpha, \beta$ correlate with
    standard monolingual competence proxies (FLORES corpus perplexity
    and Multi-BLiMP grammatical-acceptability accuracy) in the expected
    direction.
  \item Monolingual competence proxies directly predict BLEU at the
    pair level. Multi-BLiMP accuracy explains up to $44\%$ of BLEU
    variance on its own; FLORES PPL is a substantially weaker proxy.
  \item Source ($\alpha$) and target ($\beta$) effects contribute
    asymmetrically to BLEU. Target-language competence dominates for
    Llama-3.1-8B; for Aya-23-8B the two roles are more balanced.
  \item Interaction terms between source and target proxies are not
    statistically significant under any model, transform, or subset we
    tested. This means BLEU is well captured by main effects of
    per-language competence, with no detectable language-pair-specific
    component.
\end{enumerate}

The data are pairwise BLEU scores and per-language proxy metrics for
two open multilingual decoder models, Llama-3.1-8B and Aya-23-8B, over
24 languages from FLORES (552 directed pairs per model). Multi-BLiMP
accuracy is available for 17 of those languages.

For the per-language correlation, direct BLEU prediction, source--target
asymmetry, and interaction-term analyses
(Sections~\ref{sec:correlations}--\ref{sec:interaction}), we restrict
to the 17-language subset for which both proxies are available, so that
all comparisons between FLORES PPL and Multi-BLiMP are on identical
data. Corresponding results on the broader 24-language FLORES set
(and on the 23-language no-English variant) for FLORES PPL are
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:ppl-no-eng}.

We pay particular attention to a methodological subtlety throughout:
English is a leverage point that distorts FLORES-PPL-based analyses for
Llama, but not for Multi-BLiMP-based analyses. Main-body random-effects
results therefore exclude English (Section~\ref{sec:additive});
extended results with all languages and the English-outlier evidence
are in Appendix~\ref{app:english}.

\section{Setup}

\paragraph{Models.}
\code{Llama-3.1-8B} (Meta) and \code{Aya-23-8B} (Cohere). Both are dense
multilingual decoder LMs of comparable size.

\paragraph{BLEU.}
For each ordered language pair $(i, j)$ with $i \ne j$, we use the
sentencepiece-tokenized BLEU score of the model translating from
language $i$ into language $j$ on FLORES devtest. This gives a
$24 \times 24$ matrix per model with $24$ undefined diagonal cells
($552$ valid cells per model).

\paragraph{Monolingual competence proxies.}
We consider two per-language scalars:
\begin{itemize}
  \item FLORES corpus perplexity $P_i$: token-level PPL of the model on
    the FLORES devtest sub-corpus for language $i$.
  \item Multi-BLiMP accuracy $a_i$: proportion of minimal pairs in the
    Multi-BLiMP grammatical-acceptability dataset for language $i$ that
    the model scores correctly. Available for 17 of 24 languages:
    \code{ces, deu, ell, eng, fas, fra, heb, hin, ita, nld, pol, por,
    ron, rus, spa, tur, ukr}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{$R^2$ on centered scale.}
Throughout we report standard regression $R^2 = 1 - \sum_{ij}(y_{ij} -
\hat y_{ij})^2 / \sum_{ij}(y_{ij} - \bar y)^2$, where $\bar y$ is the
sample mean over the observed cells. The denominator is centered, so
the grand mean is not credited as ``explained''.

\section{An additive random-effects model fits BLEU well}
\label{sec:additive}

We fit the two-way additive random-effects model
\begin{equation}
\code{BLEU}_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_j + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad
\sum_i \alpha_i = \sum_j \beta_j = 0,
\label{eq:re}
\end{equation}
to all observed (non-diagonal) cells of the BLEU matrix by OLS. The
recovered $\alpha_i$ is a per-language source-side effect and $\beta_j$
is a per-language target-side effect, both measured as deviations from
the grand mean BLEU.

\paragraph{English is a leverage point.}
On the full 24-language set, English is a large positive outlier in
both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (Appendix~\ref{app:english}). Its presence
substantially distorts downstream PPL-based correlation analyses for
Llama. We therefore report the random-effects fit on the 23-language
subset that excludes English. Doing so does not change the headline
conclusion --- the additive fit is essentially as strong with English
included as without --- but it lets the per-language structure speak
more cleanly across the remaining languages.

\paragraph{Fit quality.}
On the 23-language no-English subset ($n = 506$ observed pairs per
model), the additive model explains $86$--$93\%$ of centered BLEU
variance with only $2(n_{\text{lang}} - 1) = 44$ free parameters per
language axis (Table~\ref{tab:additive}). With $1\%$--$2\%$ exception
(Aya $\bar y$ shifts $10.81 \to 9.88$, Llama $13.27 \to 12.02$), the
recovered $\mu$ and the additive $R^2$ are essentially indistinguishable
from those on the full set.

model & $\bar y$ & $R^2_{\text{additive}}$ \\
\midrule
Aya-23-8B    & 9.88  & $0.862$ \\
Llama-3.1-8B & 12.02 & $0.927$ \\

\paragraph{Multiplicative comparison.}
The natural alternative to an additive decomposition is multiplicative:
$\code{BLEU}_{ij} \approx u_i v_j$, the rank-1 SVD approximation. To
compare apples-to-apples we add a constant grand-mean offset to the
multiplicative model so both have the mean given for free, and we
parameter-match: additive uses $\mu + \alpha + \beta$ with sum-to-zero
constraints; multiplicative uses $\bar y + u_i v_j$ with the rank-1
factors fit by SVD of the mean-centered matrix.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
model & additive ($\mu + \alpha + \beta$)
      & multiplicative ($\bar y + u v$)
      & AMMI (additive $+$ rank-1 of residuals) \\
\midrule
Aya-23-8B    & $0.862$ & $0.716$ & $0.906$ \\
Llama-3.1-8B & $0.927$ & $0.866$ & $0.956$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Centered $R^2$ for additive vs.\ multiplicative
decompositions, and for AMMI (additive main effects plus rank-1 of
the additive residuals), on the 23-language no-English subset.
Additive and multiplicative use comparable parameter budgets;
AMMI uses roughly twice as many.}
\label{tab:add-vs-mul}
\end{table}

Three conclusions follow from Table~\ref{tab:add-vs-mul}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Additive is the better-fitting class on this parameter
    budget.} Multiplicative fits the data well but does not outperform
    additive on either model. For Aya the gap is substantial
    ($+14.6$ pp); for Llama it is smaller ($+6.1$ pp) but still favors
    additive.
  \item \textbf{Multiplicative is not negligible.} A rank-1 outer
    product captures $72$--$87\%$ of centered variance; the per-language
    structure of BLEU is not orthogonal to a multiplicative
    parameterization.
  \item \textbf{Multiplicative residual interaction is small.} AMMI
    --- adding rank-1 of the additive residuals on top of the additive
    fit --- improves $R^2$ by only $2.9$ pp (Llama) and $4.4$ pp (Aya).
    Beyond per-language main effects, genuinely multiplicative
    structure is a small contribution.
\end{enumerate}

For the rest of the paper we use the $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_j$ estimated
from the additive model in equation~\eqref{eq:re} as per-language
``competence'' estimates: $\alpha_i$ is how much above (or below) the
grand-mean BLEU language $i$ scores as a translation source, and
$\beta_j$ analogously as a target.

\section{Per-language effects align with monolingual competence proxies}
\label{sec:correlations}

Both FLORES corpus perplexity $P_i$ and Multi-BLiMP accuracy $a_i$ are
per-language scalars meant to measure model competence in language $i$.
If the additive language effects $\alpha, \beta$ recovered in
Section~\ref{sec:additive} reflect translation-relevant competence, we
expect them to correlate with each proxy in the appropriate direction
(negative with $P$, positive with $a$).

We compute these correlations on the 17-language Multi-BLiMP subset
(which includes English, since English is not an outlier in the
Multi-BLiMP framework --- Appendix~\ref{app:english}). This subset
gives the apples-to-apples comparison of the two proxies on the same
languages. Corresponding results on the 23-language non-English subset
for FLORES PPL are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:ppl-no-eng}, and they
are qualitatively consistent with what is reported here.

Table~\ref{tab:headline-corr} summarizes the strongest correlation
under each transform family for each model and role.

\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{llccc|cc}

   & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Multi-BLiMP accuracy ($|r|$)} \\
model & role & raw $P$ & $\log P$ & $1/P$ & raw $a$ & logit$(a)$ \\
\midrule
Aya-23-8B    & src ($\alpha$) & $0.46$ & $0.43$ & $0.39$ & $0.74$ & $\mathbf{0.82}$ \\
Aya-23-8B    & tgt ($\beta$)  & $0.15$ & $0.14$ & $0.14$ & $0.63$ & $\mathbf{0.77}$ \\

Bold marks the strongest correlation in each row.}
\label{tab:headline-corr}

Three observations:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Multi-BLiMP correlations are systematically stronger
    than FLORES PPL correlations.} For Aya, the best Multi-BLiMP
    correlation reaches $|r| = 0.82$ on $\alpha$ ($p < 0.001$ at $n =
    17$); the best PPL correlation is $|r| = 0.46$. For Llama,
    Multi-BLiMP reaches $|r| = 0.64$ on $\beta$ ($p < 0.01$); PPL
    reaches $|r| = 0.40$.
  \item \textbf{The correlation patterns differ between the two
    models.} For Aya, $\alpha$ tracks both proxies more strongly than
    $\beta$ does. For Llama, $\beta$ tracks both proxies more strongly
    than $\alpha$ does. We return to this asymmetry in
    Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}.
  \item \textbf{Llama $\alpha$ is the weak point.} Even Multi-BLiMP
    achieves only $|r| \approx 0.38$ for Llama source effects, vs.\
    $|r| \approx 0.64$ for Llama target effects. We interpret this as
    target-language generation being more directly downstream of the
    grammatical competence Multi-BLiMP measures, while source-language
    processing involves more content understanding.
\end{enumerate}

Full tables across all four transforms tested for each proxy are in
Appendix~\ref{app:corr-tables}, along with significance test results.

\section{Monolingual competence proxies predict BLEU directly}
\label{sec:bleu-prediction}

We now ask whether monolingual proxies predict the pairwise BLEU
score $y_{ij}$ directly, fitting
\begin{equation}
y_{ij} = a_0 + b \cdot f(\mathrm{proxy}_i) + c \cdot g(\mathrm{proxy}_j)
       + \varepsilon_{ij}
\label{eq:direct}
\end{equation}
by OLS on all valid pairs in the 17-language subset (272 pairs for
Llama, 240 for Aya after dropping pairs missing either proxy). For
PPL we test the raw-$P$ form and a log--log analog of
equation~\eqref{eq:direct}; for Multi-BLiMP we test raw-$a$ and the
log-error-rate analog $\log(1-a)$.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
specification & Aya $R^2$ & Llama $R^2$ \\
\midrule
$\code{BLEU} \sim P_s + P_t$                                 & $0.061$ & $0.071$ \\
$\log\code{BLEU} \sim \log P_s + \log P_t$                   & $0.068$ & $0.127$ \\
$\code{BLEU} \sim a_s + a_t$                                 & $\mathbf{0.394}$ & $\mathbf{0.250}$ \\
$\log\code{BLEU} \sim \log(1-a_s) + \log(1-a_t)$             & $\mathbf{0.444}$ & $\mathbf{0.339}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Direct prediction of (log-)BLEU from per-language competence
proxies, on the 17-language Multi-BLiMP subset. Both PPL coefficients
are highly significant ($p < 10^{-4}$) for Aya in both PPL forms; for
Llama the $\log P_s$ coefficient is not significant ($p = 0.38$ in the
log specification), while $\log P_t$ is highly significant ($p <
10^{-9}$). Both Multi-BLiMP coefficients are highly significant ($p
< 10^{-2}$) in every cell.}
\label{tab:direct}
\end{table}

Two takeaways from Table~\ref{tab:direct}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Multi-BLiMP is a strong predictor of BLEU.} The best
    Multi-BLiMP-based specification explains $44\%$ (Aya) and $34\%$
    (Llama) of pairwise BLEU variance using just two per-language
    scalars and no interaction. These are large effects for a model
    with only two free coefficients beyond an intercept.
  \item \textbf{FLORES PPL is a moderate predictor.} The best PPL
    specification reaches only $R^2 = 0.07$ (Aya) and $R^2 = 0.13$
    (Llama) on the same subset. Multi-BLiMP yields $3$--$6\times$ the
    explanatory power of PPL on the same data.
\end{enumerate}

The gap between PPL and Multi-BLiMP is consistent with the
per-language correlation results of Section~\ref{sec:correlations}:
the same per-language structure that drives Multi-BLiMP's stronger
$|r|$ with $\alpha, \beta$ shows up here as stronger pairwise
predictive power.

Coefficient signs are in the expected direction throughout: higher PPL
or lower Multi-BLiMP accuracy on either side of the translation pair
predicts lower BLEU. We report per-coefficient values and standard
errors in Appendix~\ref{app:coef}.

\section{Source vs.~target competence}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

The per-language effects $\alpha$ (source) and $\beta$ (target) are
both informative about BLEU, but they are not equally informative, and
their relative roles differ between the two models.

\paragraph{Variance.}
Across pairs, the variance of $\beta$ over the BLEU matrix is
substantially larger than the variance of $\alpha$ for both models:
roughly $4 \times$ for Aya and $9 \times$ for Llama. Choosing a
different target language moves BLEU substantially more than choosing
a different source language. This means that, in any per-pair
regression of BLEU on proxies, the target side has more variance to
absorb and tends to dominate the fit.

\paragraph{Correlation with competence proxies.}
On the 17-language Multi-BLiMP subset
(Table~\ref{tab:headline-corr}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Llama is target-dominant.} $\beta$ correlates with
    Multi-BLiMP at $|r| = 0.64$ (logit transform), vs.\ $|r| = 0.38$
    for $\alpha$. With FLORES PPL, the pattern is the same in
    direction: $|r| = 0.40$ for $\beta$ vs.\ $|r| = 0.13$ for $\alpha$.
  \item \textbf{Aya is more symmetric.} Multi-BLiMP correlations are
    comparable on the two sides: $|r| = 0.82$ for $\alpha$ vs.\ $|r| =
    0.77$ for $\beta$. With PPL the asymmetry shifts the other way
    ($|r| = 0.46$ for $\alpha$ vs.\ $|r| = 0.15$ for $\beta$), but
    both sides remain in the same range under Multi-BLiMP.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Translation as a target task is dominated by generating
grammatically-well-formed output in the target language; this is
exactly the kind of grammatical competence that Multi-BLiMP measures.
Translation as a source task additionally requires content
understanding, which a per-language grammaticality test does not
measure. The fact that Llama shows a stronger target/source gap than
Aya is consistent with the two models having different multilingual
training distributions; we do not attempt to attribute the difference
further here.

\section{Interaction terms are not significant}
\label{sec:interaction}

A multiplicative interaction term in equation~\eqref{eq:direct} ---
the simplest way to introduce language-pair-specific structure into
the regression --- would be evidence against the strict ``main
effects only'' reading of H1. We test for it directly.

Specifically, on the 17-language Multi-BLiMP subset (and separately
on its no-English sub-subset), we fit the saturated model
\begin{equation}
y_{ij} = a_0 + b \cdot f(\mathrm{proxy}_i) + c \cdot g(\mathrm{proxy}_j)
       + d \cdot f(\mathrm{proxy}_i) \cdot g(\mathrm{proxy}_j)
       + \varepsilon_{ij}
\label{eq:interaction}
\end{equation}
and compare it to the main-effects-only version
(equation~\eqref{eq:direct}) via a nested-model $F$-test for the
coefficient $d$.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{llccc}
\toprule
subset & predictor (model) & $F(1, \mathrm{df})$ & $p$-value & $\Delta R^2$ \\
\midrule
17-lang (Multi-BLiMP) & Multi-BLiMP, Aya   & $0.00$ & $0.99$ & $<\!10^{-4}$ \\
17-lang (Multi-BLiMP) & Multi-BLiMP, Llama & $0.56$ & $0.46$ & $0.001$ \\
17-lang (Multi-BLiMP) & PPL, Aya           & $0.09$ & $0.76$ & $<\!10^{-3}$ \\
17-lang (Multi-BLiMP) & PPL, Llama         & $0.01$ & $0.92$ & $<\!10^{-3}$ \\
\midrule
16-lang (no English)  & Multi-BLiMP, Aya   & $0.11$ & $0.75$ & $0.0003$ \\
16-lang (no English)  & Multi-BLiMP, Llama & $0.43$ & $0.51$ & $0.001$ \\
16-lang (no English)  & PPL, Aya           & $0.001$ & $0.97$ & $<\!10^{-4}$ \\
16-lang (no English)  & PPL, Llama         & $0.16$ & $0.69$ & $0.001$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Nested-model $F$-test for the source--target interaction
coefficient $d$ in equation~\eqref{eq:interaction}, on the
17-language Multi-BLiMP subset (top) and the same subset with English
removed (bottom). The interaction is uniformly non-significant.}
\label{tab:interaction}
\end{table}

The interaction $F$-test fails to reject the null in every cell of
Table~\ref{tab:interaction}: the smallest $p$-value is $0.46$, and
the increment $\Delta R^2$ from adding the interaction term is at
most $0.001$. Adding interaction terms in the raw vs.~log form, on
the full 24-language vs.~the 17-language subset, with or without
English, with PPL or Multi-BLiMP --- none of these surfaces detectable
pair-specific structure beyond what main effects already capture.

This is direct evidence for H1: pairwise BLEU is well predicted by
\emph{main effects} of per-language competence, and the additional
predictive power from a pair-specific interaction is below the noise
threshold of our data.

\section{Discussion}

The evidence reviewed here supports a strong form of H1: pairwise BLEU
scores between language pairs are well captured by an additive
decomposition into per-language source and target effects, those
effects align with monolingual competence proxies in the expected
direction, and a per-language proxy alone predicts pairwise BLEU up
to $R^2 = 0.44$. Adding language-pair-specific interaction terms does
not improve the fit. To the extent there is any residual structure
beyond per-language main effects, it is small (a few percentage
points of $R^2$, captured by AMMI's rank-1 residual).

The most surprising and arguably most actionable finding is the gap
between FLORES corpus PPL and Multi-BLiMP as competence proxies.
Multi-BLiMP achieves $3$--$6\times$ the predictive $R^2$ of PPL on the
same pairs. This suggests that, for the purpose of predicting
translation quality, a targeted measure of grammatical competence is
substantially more informative than the more commonly reported
corpus-level PPL.

\section{English as an outlier in PPL-based analyses}
\label{app:english}

This appendix documents the leverage effect of English on
FLORES-PPL-based correlations, and shows that English is \emph{not} a
leverage point in Multi-BLiMP-based analyses.

\paragraph{English in the additive fit.}
After fitting equation~\eqref{eq:re} on the full 24-language set, the
recovered $\alpha$ and $\beta$ for English are large positive
outliers: $\alpha_{\text{eng}} = +8.47$ and $\beta_{\text{eng}} =
+21.07$ for Llama; $\alpha_{\text{eng}} = +7.62$ and $\beta_{\text{eng}}
= +13.46$ for Aya (signed residuals to the linear fit of each effect
on $\log P$). These are substantially larger in magnitude than the
next-largest residuals, which range from $4$ to $12$ in absolute
value.

Table~\ref{tab:eng-effect} shows the Pearson correlation between
$\alpha, \beta$ and PPL transforms with and without English in the
sample.

For Llama, removing English roughly doubles the magnitude of Pearson
correlations across all PPL transforms and brings $r(\alpha, P)$ to
statistical significance ($p = 0.035$). For Aya the effect is small
($+1$ to $+8$ pp): the Aya signal was already systematic across all
24 languages.

\paragraph{Direct BLEU regression.}
The same outlier effect appears in the direct PPL-to-BLEU regression
on the full 24-language pair set: removing English moves Llama from
$R^2 = 0.021$ (full set) to $R^2 = 0.114$ (no English), a
$5.5\times$ improvement, with both PPL coefficients becoming highly
significant. Aya improves modestly: $R^2 = 0.105 \to 0.120$.

\paragraph{English is not an outlier for Multi-BLiMP.}
By contrast, the analogous correlations using Multi-BLiMP accuracy
are essentially unchanged when English is removed from the 17-language
subset: Aya $|r(\alpha, \text{logit}\,a)|$ shifts $0.82 \to 0.77$;
Llama $|r(\beta, \text{logit}\,a)|$ shifts $0.64 \to 0.49$. Removing
English even slightly weakens some Llama Multi-BLiMP correlations,
because English is a valid high-accuracy, high-BLEU point that
anchors the relationship. Multi-BLiMP correctly orders English with
its high BLEU; FLORES corpus PPL does not.

This contrast is the methodological justification for two choices in
the main body: (i) random-effects results in
Section~\ref{sec:additive} exclude English, so that the per-language
effects are reported on a sample where PPL-based downstream analyses
will not be artificially weakened; (ii) downstream analyses in

17-language Multi-BLiMP subset (which includes English), since in that

\section{Model specifications and inferential procedures}
\label{app:methods}

\subsection{Additive random-effects model}

Fit by ordinary least squares on the long-format pair data, with
dummy coding for source and target language identifiers. To recover
sum-to-zero $\alpha, \beta$ from a non-singular design we drop one
source dummy and one target dummy as references, then re-center the
recovered coefficients post-hoc by subtracting the mean of each
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ vector and absorbing those means into $\mu$.

Parameter count on $n_{\text{lang}}$ languages with sum-to-zero
constraints: $1 + (n_{\text{lang}} - 1) + (n_{\text{lang}} - 1) =
2 n_{\text{lang}} - 1$. For $n_{\text{lang}} = 23$ this is 45; for
$n_{\text{lang}} = 17$ this is 33. The residual degrees of freedom
for the 23-language fit are $506 - 45 = 461$; for the 17-language
fit, $272 - 33 = 239$ (Llama) or $240 - 33 = 207$ (Aya, after dropping
pairs with missing PPL).

\subsection{Multiplicative (rank-1) decomposition}

The matrix is mean-centered to $M_{\text{c}} = M - \bar y$ on its
observed cells; missing diagonal cells are imputed with column means
prior to SVD. The rank-1 SVD $u_1 v_1^\top$ of $M_{\text{c}}$ gives the
multiplicative factors. The model prediction for cell $(i, j)$ is
$\bar y + (u_1)_i (v_1)_j$. Parameter count (with the scale absorbed
once) is $2 n_{\text{lang}} - 1$, matching the additive model. We
evaluate $R^2$ on the observed cells only.

\subsection{AMMI}

After fitting the additive model, we compute the residual matrix
$M - (\mu + \alpha + \beta)$ on all observed cells (with the same
diagonal imputation as above for the SVD step), take its rank-1 SVD,
and add this back to the additive prediction. Total parameter count is
roughly twice the additive model's.

\subsection{$F$-test for the source--target interaction term}

For a nested-model comparison with one additional parameter:
\[
F = \frac{(\mathrm{RSS}_{\text{add}} - \mathrm{RSS}_{\text{int}}) / 1}
        {\mathrm{RSS}_{\text{int}} / (n - p_{\text{int}})}
   \sim F(1, n - p_{\text{int}})
\]
where $p_{\text{int}}$ is the number of free parameters in the
interaction model and $n$ is the number of observed pairs. The
$F$-test is equivalent to a two-sided $t$-test on the interaction
coefficient when only one parameter is added.

\subsection{Per-coefficient inference}

For coefficient $\hat\beta_k$ from an OLS fit with residual variance
$\hat\sigma^2 = \mathrm{RSS} / (n - p)$ and design matrix $X$, the
standard error is $\widehat{SE}(\hat\beta_k) = \hat\sigma \sqrt{(X^\top
X)^{-1}_{kk}}$ and the $t$-statistic is $\hat\beta_k /
\widehat{SE}(\hat\beta_k)$. Two-sided $p$-values are computed against
a $t$-distribution with $n - p$ degrees of freedom.
